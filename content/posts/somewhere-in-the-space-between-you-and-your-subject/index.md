<p><em>This week features a note from <a href="https://twitter.com/therealadam">Adam Keys</a>; friend, Uncommon founding member, and all-around good guy. He writes software and <a href="http://therealadam.com/">smart things on the internet</a> from Austin, and provides great insight into music, comedy, and furry creatures upon request.</em></p><p>I expected two years of improv classes would teach me a lot about performing and comedy, but I didn’t expect to learn so much about myself and how I’m different from everyone else in the process.</p><p>Improv taught me to approach things from a positive footing. You've possibly heard the improv principle of "Yes, and…". In short, if you and I are in a scene and I say "That's a lovely tree sloth you have there!", you should  say something like, "Yes, Arnold the Sloth has been my life partner since we met two years ago", rather than something like, "No, this isn't a sloth. Why would you think it's a sloth?" Building off your scene partners with a positive attitude makes it much easier to create an amusing or compelling story. That doesn't mean all improv scenes consist of the players vigorously agreeing with each other; rather, they're trying to work together instead of at odds.</p><p>Adopting this attitude wherever I can in life has helped me see past differences in opinion and miscommunications. I may not immediately agree with someone, but positive baby steps down empathy lane help me find common ground, resisting my rational impulse to tell them they're WRONG WRONG WRONG.</p><p><strong>Lesson number one:</strong> finding ways to agree is better than scoring one for yourself in the snark column.</p><p>In my improv classes, not everyone is in it for the laughs. Having done standup a few times, grown up on sitcoms and comedies, and possessing a special spot in my heart for <em>Saturday Night Live</em>, my metric for success is laughs. And yet, lots of people are in it for other things: drama (in the theatrical sense), story, the theater, genre, even art. Faced with someone not playing for laughs, I thought "What are you doing?", but later realized, "Oh; art."</p><p><strong>Lesson number two:</strong> everyone's coming from a different angle, so check your assumptions.</p><p>Finally, improv has shown me, much to my surprise, that I’m a relatively square guy. I graduated high school, went to college, got a job, bought a car and a house, got married, filled the house with a bunch of dogs and so on. The people I've met through improv served in the Peace Corps, lived abroad in Italy, Armenia, and Colombia, wait tables, freelance as writers, work in TV commercials, run theaters, teach in schools, or run after school programs for minority children. It's easy to get caught up with high-functioning peers in the communities around design, development, startups, technology, and media. In fact, there's an amazing spectrum of how people have chosen their lives and how they've responded to the peaks and valleys that they've encountered.</p><p><strong>Lesson number three:</strong> my normal is not anyone else's normal, no matter how many people around me seem similar.</p><p>Even if you're the most introverted, stage frightened, unfunny person on the planet, you’ll get a lot out of an improv class. The first class at any theater or school is designed to get you out of your head, out of your skin, and to find a shared story with all kinds of people, no stage time required. It’s a a great way to spend an evening. — <em>Adam</em></p><h3 id="prompted">Prompted</h3><p><a href="/uncommon/story/your-favorite-song-on-a-busy-street">Last week's dispatch</a> asked, <em>Who was the first friend to help you see the world differently?</em></p><p><a href="https://twitter.com/ResonanceGlobal">Radhika</a> wrote:</p><blockquote>My friend JC was the first friend to help me see the world differently.
